Commercial Water Damage in Tewksbury, MA

Water extraction, structural drying and cleanup for commercial buildings, with a written scope before work starts.

☎ Call (978) 252-8637

Commercial water damage in Tewksbury, MA is a bigger version of the same problem a homeowner faces: water is in a place it should not be, and every hour it sits, more material absorbs it. What changes is the footprint. Floor plates are larger, walls are shared, and one burst line can reach three suites before anyone opens the door.

We are a dispatch network of licensed and insured restoration professionals. We extract standing water, dry the structure, clean up and document the loss. The plumber, roofer or sprinkler contractor fixes the source, and we work around them. If a residential property is your concern instead, see water damage cleanup.

Wet ceiling tiles, soaked carpet, water coming through a shared wall

A stained ceiling grid, a squishy carpet near the back office or a damp spot on the wall your neighbor shares are all signs that water has traveled farther than the visible mark. Stained tile usually means the leak is above it, and the wet area can run well past the stain.

That is where commercial water damage mitigation begins. A moisture meter and a thermal imaging camera map the wet area before anyone pulls a single tile, so the scope matches the real extent of the loss and not just what shows on the surface.

Why the wet area must be mapped on large buildings

The Town of Tewksbury Master Plan describes about 477 acres zoned commercial, with Route 38 (Main Street) lined by strip malls, big-box businesses and small stores, and Route 133 (Andover Street) as the other state route crossing town. Buildings along those corridors often share walls and roof lines with the unit next door.

The same plan describes a Heavy Industrial District along East Street, I-495 and Woburn Street, and names the Ames Pond Industrial Park near an I-495 exit. Those are places the Town identifies, not buildings we claim to serve in particular. The practical point is that large floor plates and multi-tenant buildings let water spread under slabs and above ceilings, so the wet area must be mapped before drying equipment is placed.

Safety steps for commercial water damage: the first hour

Your first moves protect people and limit the spread. Keep staff and customers out of any area with sagging ceiling tile, standing water near outlets or a slick floor.

  1. Stop the source if you can: close the nearest shutoff valve, or the main for the unit or building.
  2. Switch off power to wet areas at the panel only if you can reach it dry. If not, leave it and call a licensed electrician.
  3. Move inventory, files and electronics up off the floor, onto shelving or tables.
  4. Call the plumber or building maintenance to repair the line.
  5. Call us for extraction and drying. Dispatch is open around the clock.

Do not walk under bowed ceilings or use a wet-dry vacuum near energized equipment. Call 911 for fire, smoke, a gas smell or anyone hurt.

Sprinkler and fire alarm leaks are a fire protection issue

The Tewksbury Fire Prevention Office at the Center Fire Station on Main Street treats fire alarm and fire sprinkler systems as fire protection systems, and work on them generally needs a Fire Department permit. The Massachusetts fire code (527 CMR 1.00) lists impairment or disconnection of a sprinkler system, water main or hydrant as a permit item.

So if the water is coming from a sprinkler head, a sprinkler main or an alarm riser, call the Fire Department prevention office and your sprinkler contractor first. We handle the water on the floor and in the structure. We do not shut down or repair the fire protection system, and your building should not sit unprotected longer than the permit process allows.

Restaurants and food establishments: the same-day Health Department form

The Tewksbury Health Department publishes a Certification of Emergency Response for Food Establishment form. It covers water interruption, water contamination, electrical interruption, sewage backup, flood and fire, and the operator must complete it and send it to the Health Department on the day the emergency begins. The form asks whether the operator stays open or closes.

That decision belongs to the operator and the Health Department, not to us. What we can do is dry and clean the affected kitchen, dining or storage areas and give you written notes on what was wet and when, which may help you fill out the form accurately. Sewage is a separate matter, covered on our sewage backup cleanup page.

How a commercial job runs

The sequence is familiar, scaled to the building. We confirm the water category under ANSI/IICRC S500, the published water damage standard, and you can ask any company whether its crews work to it.

  1. Assess and map

    Moisture meters and a thermal imaging camera mark wet ceilings, walls and slab edges. We note any utility shutoffs and what is energized.

  2. Extract

    Truck-mounted or portable extraction units pull standing water from hard floors and carpet. Wet ceiling tile and soaked insulation come down.

  3. Dry

    Air movers push dry air across surfaces, and LGR dehumidifiers pull moisture out of the room. Readings are logged on each visit.

  4. Clean and document

    Affected surfaces are cleaned, HEPA air scrubbers run when dust or contamination is a concern, and photos and moisture logs are compiled.

You get a written scope before work begins, so the property manager, owner or tenant knows what will be removed and what stays.

Offices, shared walls, utilities and who needs to know

Office water damage tends to follow the same few paths. Water soaks suspended ceiling tiles first, then runs down walls into carpet tile or broadloom, and wicks into the bottom of drywall. Carpet in an office is often glued down over a slab, so lifting it and checking the slab is part of drying. For the carpet-specific steps, read our carpet water damage page.

Shared walls and stacked floors mean other occupants may be affected. Tell the landlord or property manager early, and ask them to notify neighboring tenants. Know where the shutoffs for the unit, the building and any sprinkler zone are, because the right valve depends on where the water is coming from.

Documentation for a business insurance claim

Commercial policies vary a great deal, and the Massachusetts Division of Insurance notes that standard homeowners and commercial business policies do not cover flood damage. Sudden and accidental water damage from a pipe or appliance is often treated differently from groundwater or river flooding, so read your policy and ask your agent or insurer what applies.

We keep photos, moisture readings, equipment logs and a written scope so you have records to send. We do not decide coverage and we do not promise payment. Notify your insurer early, and ask them before major permanent repairs begin.

What changes the cost of a commercial job

No two commercial losses cost the same, and anyone quoting before seeing the building is guessing. These are the real drivers:

  • Water category, from clean supply line water to contaminated water
  • Affected area, including how many suites, floors or tenant spaces the water reached
  • Materials involved: ceiling tile, drywall, carpet, glued flooring, insulation, stock or equipment
  • Drying days and the amount of equipment needed
  • Access limits, such as after-hours work in an occupied building
  • Rebuild scope once the structure is dry

Mistakes that make commercial losses worse

Waiting for the next business day is the most common one. Drywall and carpet start to break down quickly, and the EPA advises drying wet materials within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth. Running only fans on a wet slab, tossing documentation and reopening a wet area before readings are normal are the other mistakes we see described.

If the source is not yet repaired, drying equipment only treats the symptoms. Get the plumber in, then bring us in for water mitigation so the building returns to dry readings. Neighbors in Billerica and Wilmington can also call the same dispatch line.
