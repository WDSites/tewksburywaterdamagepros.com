Does Insurance Cover Water Damage in Tewksbury, MA?

What standard homeowners policies often cover, what they often exclude, and how to check yours.

By the Tewksbury Water Damage Pros team · 2026

☎ Call (978) 252-8637

Does insurance cover water damage in Tewksbury, MA? Sometimes. Sudden and accidental damage, such as a burst pipe, is often covered. Gradual leaks and neglect often are not, and flood usually needs its own policy. Your policy wording decides, so the answers below are about what to look for, not what you will get.

We are a restoration team, not your insurer, and we cannot promise coverage. We can show you how to read the paper and which questions to take to your agent.

Sudden and accidental versus gradual

Most standard homeowners policies cover damage that happens abruptly and by surprise. A pipe that splits at 2 a.m. fits. A fitting that has dripped behind a vanity for months usually does not, because the loss is classed as seepage or maintenance.

Insurers sort claims by cause, duration and condition of the plumbing. That is why photos of the failed part and a record of when you first noticed the water matter so much.

Appliances, supply lines and roofs

Toilet and sink supply leaks, a failed washing machine hose or an AC condensate line that overflows come up often on our calls. Whether they are covered turns on policy wording about discharge from plumbing, appliances and air conditioning systems. Read that section, not just the summary.

Roof leaks follow similar logic. Sudden wind or storm damage that opens the roof is treated differently from a worn flashing that leaked for seasons. Our water damage cleanup work begins after the water is stopped, whatever the coverage answer turns out to be.

Sewer backup and sump pump failure

Water that comes up through a drain or a failed sump pump is often excluded unless your policy has a water backup or sump pump endorsement. The Massachusetts Division of Insurance says some coverage may exist if your policy includes one. Find the endorsement on your declarations page, or ask your agent or insurer.

Sewage is Category 3 water under ANSI/IICRC S500, which means it is grossly contaminated and handled with containment and protective gear. Cleanup cost is usually higher than for a clean supply line, so knowing about the endorsement before a loss is worth the phone call.

Flood is a separate policy

The Division of Insurance states that standard homeowners and commercial business policies do not cover flood damage. FEMA says the same: flood needs a separate policy, either through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private insurer.

NFIP policies typically take effect 30 days after purchase, with exceptions such as lender-required coverage. That wait means buying a policy when the river is already rising is too late. For a flooded property, flood damage restoration is the service that applies.

Do not wait for the forecast

A 30-day wait on a new flood policy means the time to ask about coverage is before the storm. Ask your agent about both NFIP and private flood options.

How to read your policy

  1. Start with the declarations page: coverage limits, deductibles and listed endorsements.
  2. Find the section on covered causes of loss and note the wording around water, plumbing and appliances.
  3. Read the exclusions: flood, surface water, seepage, wear and tear, neglect and maintenance.
  4. Look for endorsements for water backup or sump pump failure.
  5. Check how the policy defines sudden and accidental, and what it says about repeated leakage.
  6. Note the conditions after a loss: how to report, what to protect and what to keep.

Questions to ask your agent

  • Is a sudden pipe failure covered, and what does my deductible look like for water?
  • Do I have water backup or sump pump coverage, and what is its limit?
  • How does my policy treat a leak that went unnoticed for weeks?
  • Are my belongings covered at replacement value or depreciated value?
  • Do I need a separate flood policy, and how long before it starts?
  • If my home is unlivable, what does the policy say about extra living costs?
