Basement Water Damage in Tewksbury, MA

Seepage, a failed sump pump or a window well overflow can soak a basement in hours.

☎ Call (978) 252-8637

Basement water damage in Tewksbury, MA is a common call. The U.S. Energy Information Administration's 2020 survey counted about 3.30 million New England homes with a basement, 1.52 million of them finished. That is regional, not a Tewksbury figure.

This page covers water from the ground: seepage, sump failure and window well overflow. For rising river water read flood damage restoration, and for sewage see sewage backup cleanup.

Damp walls, a wet floor edge or a sump pit that is overflowing

  • Seepage where the wall meets the floor
  • White mineral crust on foundation walls
  • A sump pit that is full, or silent when it should run
  • Water spilling over a window well sill
  • Swollen baseboard in a finished room

Each sign points to a different entry point, and a waterproofing, plumbing or grading contractor fixes that. Ours is the water already inside, which our water mitigation page explains.

How groundwater and sump failures get water inside

Saturated soil pushes water through cracks and the floor-wall joint. A sump pump lifts it out, but a power outage, stuck float or clogged intake lets the pit overflow in minutes.

Massachusetts code R401.3 calls for lots graded to drain surface water away from foundation walls, so check that soil slopes away and downspouts discharge clear. The Town's Living With Wetlands guide says wetlands provide flood control and groundwater recharge. Contact the Conservation Commission before changing storm water discharge, removing trees or regrading near wetlands.

Safety steps in a wet basement: electrical and appliances

The panel, furnace and water heater often live down there.

  1. Stay out of standing water near outlets or the panel. Call your electric utility or 911.
  2. Shut the main breaker only from dry ground.
  3. Turn off the furnace and water heater if water is near them.
  4. Smell gas? Leave and call 911 or the gas utility.

If sewer water is possible, treat it as contaminated until a professional says otherwise.

Our process for a wet basement, from pump to dry

  1. Extract standing water

    Extraction units and submersible pumps move it outside, away from the house.

  2. Map the moisture

    Moisture meters and a thermal imaging camera show how far water wicked.

  3. Remove what cannot dry

    Pad, saturated insulation and lower wall sections come out.

  4. Dry the space

    Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers run, with HEPA air scrubbers if the air is musty.

  5. Verify

    Equipment leaves when readings hit a dry target.

ANSI/IICRC S500 is the published standard. The EPA says drying within 24 to 48 hours usually prevents mold growth, and mold remediation covers what comes after.

Finished basements: what is lost and what is saved

Clean water that sat a day or less may let carpet dry in place, though pad is usually replaced. Drywall wicks upward, so crews cut a few inches above the visible line and check beyond it.

Paneling, built-ins and insulation behind walls are the slow part. If a cavity cannot be metered with the board on, a strip is removed to open it. Cardboard storage is usually lost, while plastic bins and hard goods can often be cleaned and dried.

Waterproofing contractors fix how water enters, with drain tile, wall sealing or a new sump pump. We dry the damage. Dry first, repair second, rebuild third.

Insurance, NFIP coverage and prevention

The Massachusetts Division of Insurance says standard policies do not cover flood damage, though a sump pump failure or water backup endorsement may add some. Check your declarations page.

FEMA says NFIP basement coverage reaches furnaces, water heaters, sump pumps in place, foundation elements and cleanup. It does not cover finished flooring, finished walls, paint or belongings unless you bought contents coverage.

The Massachusetts Emergency Management Agency advises backup power for sump pumps. Tewksbury's sewer regulations bar sump pump water from the sanitary sewer without the Superintendent's approval, so discharge outside.
