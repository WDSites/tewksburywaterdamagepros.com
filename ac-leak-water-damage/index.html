AC Leak Water Damage in Tewksbury, MA

Clogged condensate lines and overflowing drain pans wet ceilings in summer. We dry the damage while an HVAC technician fixes the system.

☎ Call (978) 252-8637

AC leak water damage in Tewksbury, MA is a summer problem, and it needs honest framing. Many Northeast homes cool with window units or mini-splits, not a big central system, so this is not every household's risk. When it does happen, it tends to happen in humid weather, after the unit has run for days.

The usual story is a condensate line that clogs, a drain pan that fills, and a bedroom ceiling that stains below an attic air handler or closet unit. We dry what got wet. For ceiling-specific repair, see water damage repair.

Drip from a vent, brown ring on the ceiling: what it means

An air conditioner pulls humidity out of the air, and that water has to leave through a drain line. When the line clogs with algae and sludge, the pan underneath fills and spills. The spill soaks insulation and drywall, then shows up as a spreading ring on the ceiling below.

A ceiling that is damp only when the system runs points to condensate. A drip near a supply vent can mean a frozen evaporator coil that thawed, or a duct that sweats in a hot attic. Either way, the water is real and the drywall is taking it up.

If the unit is in an attic and the ceiling below is bulging, treat it as urgent. Soaked drywall gets heavy and can come down.

Safety steps before you touch anything

  • Turn the AC off at the thermostat, then at its breaker if water reaches the unit or any wiring.
  • Place a bucket under a drip and keep people out from under a bulging ceiling.
  • Do not poke a sagging spot. Releasing the water at once can bring down a section of drywall.
  • Turn off power to any ceiling light that is wet or dripping.
  • Do not climb into a wet attic. Wet insulation and unseen wiring are a bad mix.

If a light fixture is sparking or you smell burning, leave the house and call 911. An HVAC technician fixes the cause of the leak. We handle the water that has already gone where it should not.

Where the water comes from

Clogged condensate line

Algae and debris block the drain. Water backs up in the pan and spills onto the ceiling or the floor of the closet.

Overflowing drain pan

A rusted or cracked pan lets water escape even when the line drains. The EPA advises keeping AC drip pans clean and drain lines unobstructed.

Frozen evaporator coil

Low airflow or low refrigerant ices the coil. When it thaws, a lot of water arrives at once.

Mini-split drain line

A kinked or sagging line sends water down the wall behind the indoor head.

Sweating duct

The EPA notes that uninsulated ducts can cause a moisture problem as warm humid air condenses on them.

How we dry an AC leak

Ceilings are the hard part. Water pools on top of the drywall, in the insulation, and in the joist bays. A thermal imaging camera shows the wet zone, and a moisture meter confirms it.

  1. Relieve the water safely

    We make a small controlled opening to drain a bulging ceiling, then extract with a wet vacuum.

  2. Remove wet insulation and drywall

    Soaked batts and crumbling board come out so the cavity can reach dry air.

  3. Run air movers and an LGR dehumidifier

    Dry air pushes through the joist bay. The dehumidifier works hard in a humid week.

  4. Re-scan and document

    Daily readings show when the cavity is dry enough to close.

The EPA says high indoor humidity encourages mold and suggests keeping relative humidity between 30 and 50 percent. Heavy summer air makes drying slower, which is why we use dehumidification and not only fans.

What changes the price

Cost follows damage, and not the cause. For an AC leak, these are the main factors:

  • How long the line dripped and how many days it ran unnoticed
  • Ceiling type, such as drywall, plaster or textured finish
  • Whether attic insulation is soaked or only the ceiling surface
  • Equipment days, which stretch in humid weather
  • Repair scope after drying, including paint and texture matching

Insurance and an AC leak

A sudden overflow often counts as sudden and accidental, while a slow drip that built up all summer may be treated as gradual. Your policy language decides. Ask your insurer before you assume either way.

The Massachusetts Division of Insurance advises calling your insurer or agent as soon as possible and saving every receipt. It also says to wait for an inspection before permanent repairs. The HVAC repair itself is usually a separate matter from the water damage, so keep the technician's invoice with the rest of your records.

Repeated drips, mold risk and prevention

One wet afternoon is manageable. Repeated drips into the same ceiling are not, because the drywall never fully dries between episodes. Dark spotting on the ceiling or musty air in the room means the moisture has lasted long enough for mold. Mold needs moisture to grow, and remediation follows the ANSI/IICRC S520 standard, which you can ask any company about. Our mold remediation page explains the process, and health questions go to your physician.

  • Have the condensate line flushed before the season starts
  • Check the drain pan under an attic unit once a month in summer
  • Add a float switch that shuts the unit off when the pan fills
  • Insulate exposed ducts in hot attics
