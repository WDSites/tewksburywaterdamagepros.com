Hardwood Floor Water Damage Repair in Tewksbury, MA

Choosing between refinishing, replacing boards and starting over, once the floor is dry.

By the Tewksbury Water Damage Pros team · 2026

☎ Call (978) 252-8637

Hardwood floor repair after water damage comes down to one question: did the wood and the subfloor under it dry, and did the boards flatten out? In Tewksbury, MA, callers often describe cupping that does not go away weeks after a leak. That is the point where repair decisions begin.

This post is about the repair side. It assumes the leak is fixed and the floor has been dried with proper equipment. If you are still dealing with wet boards, drying comes first, because refinishing a floor that is still damp locks the problem in.

Read the floor before you choose a repair

Cupping means board edges rise higher than the centers. Crowning is the reverse. Both come from moisture moving through the wood unevenly. Gaps between boards and black stains at the seams tell a different story: gaps mean the wood has shrunk after drying too hard, and black marks mean water sat long enough for stains to set.

Give the floor time. Wood that has been dried can keep flattening for weeks. A pin-type moisture meter showing readings that match an unaffected room, and boards that sit flat when you run a straightedge across them, are the signs it is ready for work.

Sand and refinish: when it works

Sanding takes the top off the cupped edges and levels the surface. It works when the floor has dried, the boards are solid, and enough wear layer remains. A floor that was sanded several times in the past may not have much left, and a very cupped floor sanded too early can end up with flat tops and thin edges.

Refinishing also lets you even out color, fill small gaps and apply a new coat. Disturbing more than 6 square feet of painted interior surface triggers lead-safe renovation rules in Massachusetts, which is relevant for trim and baseboards you touch during the job.

Replacing boards

Spot replacement suits a clean, limited area, such as a stripe along a dishwasher or a patch near a toilet. Crews cut out the damaged boards, check the subfloor under them, and weave in new ones so the joints stagger with the old. After installation the new boards need sanding and finish to blend.

The catch is color. Aged oak and maple darken and the new strips look lighter, even after staining. Many homeowners decide to refinish the whole room so the transition falls at a doorway instead of in the middle of the floor.

The subfloor check nobody sees

Before any new wood goes down, someone should check what is underneath. Plywood and OSB subfloors swell, delaminate and can stay wet long after the surface boards feel dry. A meter reading on the subfloor, taken after a board is lifted, is the only reliable test.

A damp subfloor is a mold risk once it is covered again. The state mold guide says to find and fix the source of moisture and that areas of more than 10 square feet are a professional job. Hidden mold in a subfloor is exactly the case the guide describes as a reason to call a pro.

When to call

Call a professional if the floor keeps cupping after several weeks, if you smell mustiness from under the boards, if the subfloor feels soft, or if the water came from sewage or a river. Contaminated water means Category 3, and the affected flooring is usually removed.

Cost factors for hardwood repair

Repair cost follows the scope. Square footage, species and finish, how much of the floor is damaged, whether the subfloor needs replacing, how many rooms must be blended, and whether the original drying is documented all matter. Moving furniture and appliances adds labor.

Local note

The March 2010 flood record and what it means for floors

The National Weather Service Boston report on the March 2010 floods records 10 to 20 inches of rain across eastern Massachusetts that month. The rivers around Tewksbury hit records: the Shawsheen River at Wilmington reached 10.59 feet on March 16, and the Concord River at Lowell reached 9.74 feet in mid-March.

The report does not name Tewksbury, so we do not claim the town flooded. The practical point is that river flood water is Category 3, and hardwood touched by it is usually replaced, not refinished. Standard homeowners policies do not cover flood, so check the FEMA Flood Map Service Center for your address and ask your insurer about separate flood coverage.
